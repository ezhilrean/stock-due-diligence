# Stock Due-Diligence Checklist

Use this as an investigation checklist, not as a claim that every item has been conclusively cleared. Mark each item `Reviewed`, `Flag`, `Not found in sources reviewed`, `Not applicable`, or `Not verified`. A checked item means it was reviewed, not necessarily cleared.

## A. Identity and business
- [ ] Confirm legal entity, ticker, exchange, share class, and listing jurisdiction.
- [ ] Confirm reporting currency, fiscal year, analysis date, and financial periods.
- [ ] Understand company history, products, services, revenue model, customers, and markets.
- [ ] Review founders, management, major shareholders, subsidiaries, joint ventures, and related parties.
- [ ] Identify major customers, suppliers, competitors, geographic markets, and dependencies.
- [ ] Assess dependence on a single customer, supplier, product, or key person.
- [ ] Assess competitive advantages, barriers to entry, reputation, and business sustainability.

## B. Financial health and accounting
- [ ] Review multi-year revenue, operating profit, net profit, margins, and growth trends (5–10 years where available).
- [ ] Review operating cash flow and free cash flow.
- [ ] Examine cash reserves, debt, interest coverage, liquidity, balance-sheet strength, and working capital.
- [ ] Compare accounts receivable with revenue and inventory growth with sales.
- [ ] Read auditor opinions, qualifications, material weaknesses, and auditor changes.
- [ ] Check restatements, accounting-policy changes, unusual adjustments, and unexplained expenses.
- [ ] Compare reported profit with cash generation over time.
- [ ] Review stock-based compensation, repeated share issuance, and dilution.
- [ ] Investigate unusual transactions, guarantees, off-balance-sheet commitments, and liabilities.
- [ ] Review capital expenditure, return on invested capital, and funding needs.
- [ ] Assess whether growth depends on repeated fundraising or borrowing.

**Key test:** Reported profits should ideally be supported by real cash generation over time. Persistent gaps deserve investigation.

## C. Legal and regulatory
- [ ] Search current and historical civil litigation and shareholder actions.
- [ ] Check criminal investigations, prosecutions, charges, and convictions.
- [ ] Review securities, accounting, bribery, corruption, and insider-trading matters.
- [ ] Check regulatory enforcement, fines, penalties, sanctions, licensing, and exchange-compliance issues.
- [ ] Review tax disputes, tax-evasion allegations, and unpaid tax liabilities.
- [ ] Check labor, safety, consumer-protection, environmental, competition, antitrust, and price-fixing matters.
- [ ] Review intellectual-property disputes and patent-infringement claims.
- [ ] Investigate substantiated misconduct involving employees, customers, journalists, or whistleblowers, including threats or retaliation.
- [ ] Check cases involving executives, directors, founders, and controlling shareholders.
- [ ] For each material matter, record jurisdiction, case identifier where available, date, parties, allegations, procedural status, findings, outcome, penalties or settlement, appeals, financial exposure, company response, and source.
- [ ] Distinguish allegations and filed lawsuits from established findings.

## D. Fraud, transparency, and disclosure
- [ ] Verify material claims about products, contracts, customers, revenue, and growth.
- [ ] Check for misleading financial statements, questionable revenue recognition, and unexplained inconsistencies.
- [ ] Review related-party transactions, conflicts of interest, and significant asset transfers.
- [ ] Check for undisclosed liabilities, guarantees, and material risks.
- [ ] Compare annual reports, regulatory filings, exchange announcements, company statements, and independent reporting.
- [ ] Review filing delays, disclosure gaps, unexplained reporting-date changes, and frequent accounting-policy or auditor changes.
- [ ] Review insider selling around negative announcements in context; do not infer wrongdoing from sales alone.
- [ ] Check unusual share issuance, dilution, or transactions that may benefit insiders.
- [ ] Assess credible whistleblower claims, investigative journalism, short-seller reports, and independent investigations.
- [ ] Distinguish confirmed facts, allegations, interpretations, and speculation.
- [ ] Focus on required disclosures and material omissions. Legitimate trade-secret protection or a lack of disclosure alone does not prove wrongdoing.

## E. Cybersecurity and IT integrity
- [ ] Check confirmed cybersecurity breaches, data leaks, ransomware, and other security incidents.
- [ ] Investigate credible reports of malware, spyware, unauthorized access, or manipulation only where evidence supports them.
- [ ] Assess account integrity, transaction accuracy, missing transactions, incorrect balances, and record reliability.
- [ ] Review regulatory findings, data-protection compliance, and misuse or unauthorized sharing of customer data.
- [ ] Check material weaknesses in internal controls, IT systems, and audit findings.
- [ ] Check repeated outages, system failures, and unexplained data discrepancies.
- [ ] Assess customer impact, business disruption, financial impact, remediation, and independent security assurance.
- [ ] Record the source and status of each incident.
- [ ] Do not label an incident malware or deliberate manipulation based on a technical problem, account discrepancy, or outage alone.

## F. Management and governance
- [ ] Review backgrounds of the CEO, CFO, directors, founders, and controlling shareholders.
- [ ] Check previous bankruptcies, failed businesses, regulatory sanctions, and substantiated misconduct.
- [ ] Assess board independence and effectiveness and auditor independence.
- [ ] Compare executive compensation with company performance.
- [ ] Review related-party transactions and conflicts of interest.
- [ ] Examine insider holdings, buying, selling, and disclosed transactions.
- [ ] Understand voting rights, unequal share classes, and minority-shareholder protections.
- [ ] Review whistleblower policies, internal controls, and treatment of people raising concerns.
- [ ] Compare management guidance and projections with actual results.

## G. Valuation and business outlook
- [ ] Use valuation methods appropriate to the sector, maturity, and business model.
- [ ] Consider P/E, P/S, P/B, EV/EBITDA, free-cash-flow yield, and dividend yield where appropriate.
- [ ] Compare valuation with the company's history and relevant peers.
- [ ] State valuation date, share-price source, financial period, method, and assumptions.
- [ ] Review cash-flow assumptions, capital needs, debt, and dividend sustainability.
- [ ] Estimate intrinsic value using DCF or another suitable method where appropriate.
- [ ] Consider optimistic, base, and pessimistic scenarios.
- [ ] Estimate upside, downside, sensitivity to assumptions, and margin of safety.
- [ ] Assess growth sustainability, R&D, product launches, customer retention, acquisitions, capital allocation, and dilution.
- [ ] Consider competition, disruption, regulation, macroeconomics, interest rates, inflation, currency, supply chains, geopolitics, commodity exposure, government dependence, and financially material climate risks.
- [ ] Explain valuation limitations; a low multiple alone does not prove a stock is cheap.

## H. Trading and shareholder risks
- [ ] Review historical price and trading-volume trends.
- [ ] Investigate unusual price spikes or volume before major announcements; treat patterns as leads, not proof.
- [ ] Review credible market-manipulation, pump-and-dump, or coordinated-promotion allegations with corroboration.
- [ ] Review short interest and short-seller reports with independent verification.
- [ ] Check insider-trading disclosures.
- [ ] Review share issuance, dilution, buybacks, stock splits, lock-up expirations, and major shareholder sales.
- [ ] Assess liquidity and likely transaction impact.
- [ ] Review volatility, trading halts, exchange compliance, and suspension risk.
- [ ] Examine institutional ownership and changes in major shareholders.
- [ ] Assess delisting, restructuring, and bankruptcy risk.
- [ ] Review dividend coverage and tax implications where relevant.

## I. Evidence quality and sources
- [ ] Prioritize official filings, audited financial statements, exchange disclosures, regulator records, and court records where relevant.
- [ ] Use company investor-relations material and reputable independent reporting for context.
- [ ] Treat social media, forums, and unverified claims as leads only, not proof.
- [ ] Record source links and publication/access dates for material claims.
- [ ] Verify legal and regulatory status as of the report date.
- [ ] Describe conflicting evidence, source limitations, and missing information.
- [ ] Confirm sources were actually accessed; do not invent searches, records, figures, citations, or links.
- [ ] Label evidence as confirmed fact, official allegation/proceeding, reported allegation, analysis/inference, or unknown/not verified.
- [ ] Avoid unsupported claims that a company is “clean,” “fraudulent,” or “safe.”

## J. Industry and external factors
- [ ] Assess industry growth, long-term demand, competitor strength, and market share.
- [ ] Consider new competitors, substitutes, and technological disruption, including AI where relevant.
- [ ] Review regulatory changes and government policy.
- [ ] Assess interest rates, inflation, currency exposure, economic cycles, and geopolitical risk.
- [ ] Investigate supply-chain concentration and commodity-price exposure where applicable.
- [ ] Review dependence on government contracts or subsidies.
- [ ] Consider financially material climate and environmental risks.

## K. Growth and sustainability
- [ ] Review historical revenue, earnings, and free-cash-flow growth.
- [ ] Test the credibility of management's future growth projections.
- [ ] Assess R&D effectiveness and commercial results from new products.
- [ ] Review capital expenditure, return on invested capital, and acquisition/integration risks.
- [ ] Examine customer retention and customer-acquisition costs where relevant.
- [ ] Assess margin sustainability and long-term competitive advantages.
- [ ] Check whether growth depends on repeated fundraising or borrowing.

## L. Dividends, ownership, and shareholder protection
- [ ] Review dividend history, payout ratio, and cash-flow coverage.
- [ ] Assess whether share repurchases create shareholder value.
- [ ] Track changes in outstanding shares.
- [ ] Review shareholder rights, voting restrictions, and minority protections.
- [ ] Understand parent-company and subsidiary ownership structures.
- [ ] Check foreign-ownership restrictions where applicable.
- [ ] Review delisting, suspension, bankruptcy, and restructuring risk.
- [ ] Consider tax treatment of dividends and capital gains in the investor's jurisdiction.

## Five-stage review process

1. **Verify the company:** confirm legal entity, ticker, exchange, filings, ownership, business model, and source reliability.
2. **Investigate red flags:** check fraud, accounting concerns, legal exposure, regulatory penalties, cybersecurity issues, and management misconduct.
3. **Test financial strength:** analyze earnings quality, cash flow, debt, profitability, competitive position, and growth sustainability.
4. **Estimate fair value:** estimate intrinsic value, compare it with the current share price, and calculate possible downside and upside.
5. **Document the decision:** record evidence, unresolved questions, key risks, and conditions that would change the assessment. Use a qualified status such as `Potential candidate`, `Watchlist`, `Further research`, or `Avoid / elevated risk`.

## Red flags that may require pausing the investment

- Confirmed material fraud or serious financial misrepresentation.
- An auditor refusing to give an opinion or a serious unresolved audit qualification.
- Material undisclosed liabilities or unexplained financial discrepancies.
- Serious regulatory sanctions or legal proceedings that threaten business viability.
- Credible evidence of deliberate customer-account manipulation or material cybersecurity misconduct.
- Management making demonstrably false statements or repeatedly withholding material information.
- A business that cannot explain how it generates cash or remains solvent.

An allegation is not the same as a proven violation. Assess evidence, severity, financial impact, and management response. Some findings may be disqualifying; others may be manageable if understood and reflected in valuation.

## Recommended research record

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Company / ticker | Legal name, ticker, exchange, share class |
| Date reviewed | Date the research was performed |
| Business summary | How the company makes money |
| Financial health | Revenue, profits, cash flow, debt, auditor findings |
| Legal / regulatory | Cases, status, findings, penalties, sources |
| Fraud / transparency | Verified facts, allegations, unresolved questions |
| Cybersecurity / IT | Confirmed incidents, impact, remediation |
| Governance | Management history, board, related parties, shareholder rights |
| Valuation | Metrics used, fair-value range, assumptions |
| Main risks | The most important ways the investment could lose value |
| Evidence quality | Official record, audited disclosure, credible reporting, allegation, or unknown |
| Decision | Potential candidate, watchlist, avoid / elevated risk, or further research |
| Review triggers | Events or new information that would change the conclusion |

## Three core principles

1. **Evidence first:** separate confirmed facts, allegations, opinions, and unknowns.
2. **Financial quality and fair value:** do not approve a stock based only on the absence of fraud or lawsuits.
3. **Document the conclusion:** explain why the stock may be attractive, what could go wrong, and what evidence would invalidate the investment thesis.

This checklist is a research aid, not a guarantee of safety or a personalized recommendation to buy or sell any security.
