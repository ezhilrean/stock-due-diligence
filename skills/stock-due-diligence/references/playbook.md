# End-to-End Stock Due-Diligence Playbook

Use this playbook for each stock research request. It is a research process, not a claim that every check will be conclusive.

## Step 1: Scope the request

Record:
- Company name and ticker.
- Exchange and jurisdiction.
- Whether the user is evaluating a new purchase, an existing holding, or a comparison.
- Analysis date and preferred time horizon, if supplied.
- Relevant price date, reporting currency, and financial periods.

If the ticker maps to multiple securities or the exchange is unclear, ask one focused clarifying question. Do not silently analyze the wrong company. Do not request unnecessary personal financial information.

## Step 2: Build a source plan

Start with, as relevant:
1. Latest annual report and audited financial statements.
2. Latest interim or quarterly filing.
3. Stock-exchange announcements and material-event filings.
4. Securities regulator and other relevant regulator records.
5. Official court records and enforcement announcements.
6. Company investor-relations materials.
7. Reputable independent journalism and specialist research.
8. Credible cybersecurity disclosures or technical research, if relevant.

Choose sources appropriate to the listing jurisdiction and sector. Record publication or filing dates and access dates. Do not claim a source was accessed unless it was actually reviewed.

## Step 3: Understand the business

Summarize how revenue is generated, who pays, why customers choose the product, the competitive landscape, major dependencies, and whether the business model appears sustainable. Flag material customer, supplier, product, geographic, or channel concentration risks.

## Step 4: Analyze financial quality

Review several years of data where available. Assess revenue and profit trends, margins, operating cash flow, free cash flow, debt, liquidity, working capital, receivables, inventory, dilution, auditor comments, restatements, and unusual transactions.

Avoid comparing metrics across inconsistent periods or currencies without noting the difference. Explain sector-specific limitations and distinguish reported data from calculations and estimates.

## Step 5: Investigate integrity risks

Search appropriate official sources for litigation, enforcement, sanctions, regulatory violations, tax issues, fraud findings or allegations, and material disclosure concerns. For each material issue, record:
- Nature of the matter.
- Alleged or established conduct.
- Date and jurisdiction; case identifier where available.
- Current status and outcome.
- Potential financial or operational impact.
- Company response.
- Sources and remaining uncertainty.

Treat allegations as allegations until reliable evidence establishes more. A lawsuit, investigation, settlement, or short-seller report is not by itself proof of wrongdoing.

## Step 6: Assess cybersecurity and IT risks

Prioritize confirmed incidents, official notices, customer impact, material IT-control weaknesses, remediation, and repeat incidents. Distinguish breaches, outages, data-quality problems, and control weaknesses. Do not speculate about malware, deliberate concealment, or manipulation based only on symptoms or online rumors.

## Step 7: Review management and governance

Check executive and director track records, board independence, auditor independence, related-party transactions, compensation, insider holdings and transactions, shareholder rights, internal controls, and management's history of delivering on stated goals.

## Step 8: Assess industry and trading risks

Consider competition, disruption, regulation, macroeconomic sensitivity, currencies, supply chains, geopolitical exposure, liquidity, dilution, unusual trading activity, exchange compliance, delisting, restructuring, and bankruptcy risks. Treat patterns as leads to investigate, not proof of misconduct.

## Step 9: Estimate valuation

Choose methods suitable to the company's sector and maturity. Explain assumptions and limitations. Where reasonable, construct optimistic, base, and pessimistic cases. State what drives each scenario and what evidence could invalidate it. Avoid false precision and state the price source and valuation date.

## Step 10: Synthesize the conclusion

Use a research status rather than a guaranteed instruction:
- **Potential candidate:** Evidence is broadly supportive, material risks appear manageable, and valuation may be attractive under stated assumptions.
- **Watchlist:** Interesting company, but price, timing, or unresolved questions require further review.
- **Further research:** Material evidence gaps or unresolved risks prevent a sound conclusion.
- **Avoid / elevated risk:** Evidence indicates serious risks or an unattractive risk/reward profile under the stated assumptions.

Explain why the status was chosen, the largest downside risks, and what could change the assessment. These are research labels, not personalized or guaranteed buy/sell instructions.

## Step 11: Deliver and maintain the record

Use `deliverable-template.md`. Include a source log, as-of date, and relevant price and financial-period dates. Complete `definition-of-done.md` and use `checklist.md` to track each applicable check. If a critical item cannot be completed, disclose the limitation and use `Further research` rather than implying the company passed. If monitoring or a later review is requested, specify a concrete trigger (such as an earnings filing, court decision, regulatory announcement, or valuation threshold) rather than vague general monitoring.
