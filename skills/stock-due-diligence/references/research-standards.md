# Research and Reporting Standards

## 1. Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in roughly this order, depending on the claim:
1. Official regulatory filings, audited financial statements, stock-exchange disclosures, regulator orders, court judgments, and official records.
2. Direct company statements and investor-relations materials.
3. Reputable news organizations and established specialist publications.
4. Independent research, short-seller reports, and technical analysis.
5. Social media, forums, and unverified claims as leads only—not as proof.

Primary sources can still be incomplete or self-interested. Cross-check material claims when feasible.

## 2. Evidence labels

Use clear language:
- **Confirmed fact:** Supported by authoritative records or strong corroborated evidence.
- **Official allegation / proceeding:** An authority or party has made a claim; the outcome may remain unresolved.
- **Reported allegation:** A credible secondary source reports a claim that may not be independently established.
- **Analysis / inference:** A reasoned interpretation based on disclosed evidence.
- **Unknown / not verified:** Evidence was not found or could not be confirmed in the sources reviewed.

Never convert an allegation into a finding or an absence of evidence into proof that no issue exists.

## 3. Legal and regulatory matters

For each material case, seek the jurisdiction, case or proceeding identifier where available, filing or announcement date, parties, allegations, procedural status, findings, penalties or settlement, appeals, company response, and potential financial impact. Check current status rather than relying on an old headline.

A settlement does not necessarily mean an admission of liability. A filed lawsuit does not prove the claims. Explain the actual outcome and any limitations in the record.

## 4. Financial analysis

- State the reporting period and currency for financial metrics.
- Distinguish company-reported data from calculations or estimates.
- Explain unusual accounting items and non-GAAP measures.
- Compare profit with cash flow and assess debt and liquidity.
- Use comparable periods and peer groups where possible.
- Explain limitations for financial institutions, early-stage companies, and cyclical businesses.
- Never invent missing figures or silently fill gaps with assumptions.

## 5. Valuation

- State valuation date, share price source, method, and assumptions.
- Use methods appropriate to the company's sector, maturity, and business model.
- Include sensitivity or scenarios when the result depends heavily on assumptions.
- Explain limitations and avoid presenting an estimate as an objective certainty.
- Do not use a low multiple alone as proof that a stock is cheap.
- Do not present a valuation range without explaining the key drivers.

## 6. Cybersecurity and IT integrity

Use precise terms. Distinguish confirmed breaches, service outages, data-quality issues, control weaknesses, allegations, and speculative claims. A missing account display or inaccurate balance may have multiple causes and does not alone establish malware or intent. Describe evidence, customer impact, investigation status, and remediation.

## 7. Transparency and conflicts

Check disclosure requirements and materiality for the relevant jurisdiction. A company may legitimately keep trade secrets confidential. Focus on required disclosures, material omissions, inconsistencies, related-party dealings, and evidence of deception rather than treating all non-public information as misconduct.

## 8. Risk ratings

If using labels such as Low / Medium / High, define them and explain the basis. Consider:
- Likelihood and severity.
- Financial materiality.
- Evidence strength.
- Time horizon.
- Management response and remediation.
- Whether the risk is already reflected in price.

Do not assign a precise numerical risk score without a consistent, explained method.

## 9. Responsible conclusions

The report is a research aid, not a guarantee of returns or personalized financial advice. Avoid certainty about future price movements. Make the reasoning auditable: readers should be able to trace important conclusions to evidence and assumptions.
